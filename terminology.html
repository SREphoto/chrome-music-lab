Pitch, Frequency & Octaves

Pitch & Frequency

Hertz (Hz)

Pitch is how high or low a musical sound seems to our ears. Acoustically, it is governed by Frequency — the number of complete sound wave vibrations per second, measured in Hertz (Hz). Faster vibrations produce higher pitches; slower vibrations produce lower pitches.

Octave

[AHK-tiv] • 2:1 Ratio

An Octave is the musical interval between one pitch and another with double or half its frequency. Because the wave peaks align precisely every second cycle, our brains perceive octaves as the "same" note at a different height.

Intervals, Scales & Arpeggios

Interval

Pitch Distance

An Interval is the distance in pitch between two notes. When notes sound together, it's a harmonic interval; when sounded in sequence, it's a melodic interval. Smaller intervals like half-steps and whole-steps form scales.

Interactive Experiment: Interval Explorer

Scale

Latin: Scala (Ladder)

A Scale is an organized ladder of notes spanning an octave. The formula of whole-steps and half-steps dictates the scale's emotional quality — e.g. Major feels bright and uplifting, while Minor feels reflective or dramatic.

Interactive Experiment: Scale Mode Explorer

Arpeggio

[ahr-PEJ-ee-oh] • Broken Chord

An Arpeggio (from Italian arpeggiare, meaning "to play on a harp") is a broken chord where the individual notes of a triad or seventh chord are played rapidly one after another rather than struck at the same instant.

Harmony, Chords & Consonance

Chords & Triads

Harmonic Foundation

A Chord is three or more notes sounded together. The fundamental building block is a Triad, consisting of a Root, a Third, and a Fifth. A single semitone change on the 3rd shifts a chord from cheerful Major to melancholic Minor.

Interactive Experiment: Major vs. Minor Triad

Consonance vs. Dissonance

Acoustic Stability & Tension

Consonance sounds stable, pleasant, and resolved because the frequencies share simple mathematical ratios. Dissonance produces tension and auditory "beating" because frequencies are too close and acoustically clash.

Cadence

Musical Punctuation

A Cadence is a harmonic two-chord progression that ends a musical phrase, acting like a comma or period in a sentence. The most famous is the Authentic Cadence (V-I), creating ultimate resolution and finality.

Rhythm, Meter & Tempo

Tempo (BPM)

Italian: Time / Speed

Tempo is the pace of the music, measured in Beats Per Minute (BPM). Classical Italian terms describe tempo moods: Adagio (slow, 66 BPM), Andante (walking pace, 90 BPM), Allegro (fast, 130 BPM), and Presto (very fast, 180 BPM).

Meter & Time Signature

4/4 • 3/4 • 6/8

Meter organizes pulses into recurring patterns of strong and weak beats. 4/4 (Common Time) has 4 beats per bar with an accent on 1. 3/4 (Waltz) has 3 beats (STRONG-weak-weak). 6/8 groups into two compound pulses of three.

Polyrhythm

Cross-Rhythms (3:2)

A Polyrhythm is the simultaneous sounding of two different metric subdivisions that do not easily divide into each other — such as playing 3 equal beats on one drum in the exact same time as 2 equal beats on another.

Timbre ("Tone Color")

[TAM-ber]

Timbre is the unique texture that allows you to tell a flute, piano, and violin apart even when they play the exact same pitch and volume. In electronic synthesis, timbre starts with the geometric waveform shape.

The Harmonic Series

Every acoustic note is actually a composite chord of a Fundamental frequency (f) and quieter mathematical multiples (2f, 3f, 4f...). This overtone recipe is what creates an instrument's rich acoustic character.

Dynamics & ADSR Envelopes

Dynamics

Volume & Expression

Dynamics denote variations in volume: pp (pianissimo, very soft), p (piano), mf (mezzo-forte, medium loud), f (forte, loud), and ff (fortissimo). Crescendo means gradually getting louder; Decrescendo means fading softer.

ADSR Envelope

Attack • Decay • Sustain • Release

An ADSR Envelope determines how sound volume evolves over time: Attack (initial onset), Decay (drop to sustain level), Sustain (volume while held), and Release (tail fade after release). A piano has instantaneous attack; a cello has gentle attack.

Articulation & Expression

Staccato vs. Legato

Detached vs. Connected

Staccato (Italian: "detached") instructs notes to be played short, crisp, and separate. Legato (Italian: "bound together") instructs notes to be smoothly connected and flowing without breaks.

Interactive Experiment: Articulation Comparison

Glissando & Vibrato

Glide & Pitch Wobble

Glissando is a continuous slide upwards or downwards between two pitches (like a trombone slide). Vibrato is a slight, pulsing fluctuation in pitch used by singers, violinists, and cellists to add warmth and richness.
